# Governance Debrief

## A repeatable decision framework

The cases do not all have the same answer, but the same questions improve every decision.

| Consideration | Key question |
| --- | --- |
| Identity and role | Who is requesting access, and what is the person's or organization's role? |
| Purpose | Why is the information needed? |
| Scope | Which patients, encounters, and data elements are necessary? |
| Identifiers | Are direct identifiers needed for the purpose? |
| Duration | Is access one-time, time-limited, or ongoing? |
| Location | Will data remain internal or be disclosed externally? |
| Safeguards | What technical, administrative, and contractual protections are appropriate? |
| Oversight | Who approves, monitors, audits, and ends access? |
| Competing harms | What are the risks of both inappropriate disclosure and inappropriate restriction? |

## Important distinctions

- The fact that information can be exchanged does not establish that every person should receive every element.
- The presence of sensitive information does not mean clinically appropriate exchange should automatically be blocked.
- Treatment, quality improvement, research, vendor support, and patient access may require different pathways and safeguards.
- An external organization may need a defined agreement, limited access, monitoring, or additional review.
- Governance continues after access is granted through auditing, retention limits, incident response, and reevaluation.
- Technical inconvenience and competitive preference should not be disguised as privacy or security requirements.

## Closing statement

> Good governance is not simply saying yes or no. It makes appropriate information available for a legitimate purpose while limiting unnecessary exposure and preserving accountability.

Specific institutional decisions should be reviewed under current law, regulation, policy, and organizational guidance.
